● Here's AeroMesh explained like you're a smart 18-year-old who hasn't read the
  paper.

  The problem

  When a big typhoon or earthquake hits, cell towers and fiber lines get knocked
  out. Rescue teams on the ground lose phone and internet service exactly when
  they need it most. Think Typhoon Odette hitting the Philippines in 2021, or
  the 2018 earthquake/tsunami in Palu, Indonesia. Roads get blocked, people
  can't reach the area for days, and nobody can even see how bad the damage is
  in real time.

  The idea

  Instead of waiting for engineers to drive out and fix cell towers, send a
  swarm of drones to form a temporary flying internet network over the disaster
  zone, right away. Picture a mesh Wi-Fi system, but each "router" is a drone
  hovering in the air. Some drones are called relay drones and their only job is
  to pass signal around, like repeaters. Other drones are inference drones and
  they carry a small onboard AI model that looks at camera images and tries to
  judge how badly buildings are damaged, without needing to send huge video
  files back to a command center over a weak connection.

  Why this is actually hard (not just "put drones in the sky")

  1. Drones die. Battery runs out, GPS gets jammed, one crashes. The network has
     to notice a hole opened up and automatically reposition other drones to
     patch it, with no human telling them where to go. This is called
     self-healing.
  2. Not everyone deserves equal bandwidth. A rescue team pulling someone out of
     rubble needs the connection way more than a civilian trying to check
     social media. So the system has to automatically prioritize, and the paper
     builds actual math (an auction system, like eBay bidding, where drones
     "sell" their limited bandwidth to the task that needs it most) to decide
     this fairly and fast.
  3. The onboard AI has to know when it's unsure. A tiny drone computer can't
     run a giant, maximally accurate AI model, it has to run something smaller
     and faster. So the system is built so the cheap model handles easy cases
     itself, but when an image is confusing (smoke, weird angle, debris it's
     never seen) it says "I'm not confident" and escalates that one image to a
     more powerful drone instead of silently guessing wrong. That's the
     "uncertainty-aware" part, it's a safety mechanism against an AI that's
     confidently wrong during a life-or-death assessment.

  What makes it "Southeast Asia" specific

  Instead of a generic "drones in disasters" paper, it's grounded in three real
  documented events with real regulatory rules:
  - Philippines — Typhoon Odette knocking out telecom, under actual Philippine
    aviation drone rules (CAAP)
  - Indonesia — the Palu earthquake/tsunami, where the islands themselves make
    ground access brutal, under Indonesian aviation rules (DGCA)
  - Vietnam — Typhoon Yagi flooding the Mekong region in 2024

  This matters because drone laws and disaster geography are different in every
  country, so a system that ignores that isn't realistic.

  What was actually done (the "process")

  1. Took an existing rough research concept and expanded it into a full
     academic manuscript targeting the journal Drones (MDPI).
  2. Formal math problem statement was written out properly, defining things
     like how "connected" the drone network is using graph theory (an idea from
     a 1973 paper called algebraic connectivity).
  3. Two algorithms were specified: one for self-healing mesh repair, one for
     the priority auction.
  4. A full experiment plan was written, including which public disaster-imagery
     datasets to test on (six of them, including a Southeast-Asia-specific one
     called AINDAA), what to compare against, and what "ablation studies"
     (turning off one piece at a time to see what breaks) to run.
  5. 76 real references were checked one by one against actual published papers,
     nothing was invented. A few original citations in the source document were
     flat-out wrong (wrong authors, unverifiable papers), and those were
     corrected or dropped rather than kept.
  6. Diagrams of the system architecture and the three disaster scenarios were
     built as vector graphics, delivered in both PDF and SVG.
  7. Compiled into a 24-page LaTeX document using the real MDPI journal
     template, with a matching blind (anonymous) version for peer review.

  The most important finding, and it's an unusual one

  There is no "it worked, here's the data" result yet. The Results section is
  intentionally left as labeled placeholders, not because anything failed, but
  because no actual simulation has been run. This was a deliberate choice: you
  said you'll run the real simulation yourself later, so the honest thing to do
  was write the complete, pre-registered experimental plan (exactly what will be
  measured and how) without faking any numbers to make the paper look finished.
  That's actually a mark of good scientific practice, not a weakness, papers
  that announce results before the experiment exists are the ones that get
  retracted.

  Bottom line

  AeroMesh is a plan for a drone swarm that turns itself into emergency internet
  plus an AI damage-scanner after a disaster, built specifically around real
  Southeast Asian disaster corridors and real regulations, with the full math,
  algorithms, and experiment design written up and verified, waiting on you to
  actually run the simulation and fill in the real numbers.
